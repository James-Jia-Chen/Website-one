What is Philosophy?

There are many good answers to this question. Here, I will approach it specifically from the perspective of contemporary academic philosophy.

There are many good ways to divide up contemporary philosophy, and any such classification will inevitably be somewhat crude. Still, for introductory purposes, it can be useful to think of current academic philosophy as consisting of several broad areas: metaphysics and philosophy of mind, epistemology, value theory, philosophy of language and logic, philosophy of science, history of philosophy, and a range of socially oriented or interdisciplinary fields such as feminist philosophy and philosophy of race.

These categories overlap considerably. For example, philosophy of mind often intersects with metaphysics, epistemology, and philosophy of cognitive science, while feminist philosophy and philosophy of race can involve questions in ethics, social and political philosophy, epistemology, and metaphysics. The point of this classification is therefore not to draw strict boundaries, but simply to provide a rough map of the field.

The word "philosophy" comes from the Greek "philosophia," commonly translated as "love of wisdom."

Metaphysics and Philosophy of Mind

Metaphysics studies the most general features of reality. It asks questions such as: What kinds of things exist? What are objects, properties, causes, possibilities, and laws of nature? What is the nature of time? What makes a person the same person over time?

Philosophy of mind focuses more specifically on the nature of mental states and mental life. It examines consciousness, perception, belief, desire, intention, emotion, mental representation, and the relationship between the mind and the physical world. Major questions include whether mental states can be reduced to physical states, what consciousness consists in, and how perception represents the external world.

Epistemology

Epistemology is the study of knowledge, justified belief, evidence, rationality, and understanding. It asks questions such as: What is knowledge? When is a belief justified? What should we believe given a certain body of evidence? Can we know anything about the external world?

Contemporary epistemology also includes areas such as social epistemology, formal epistemology, testimony, disagreement, skepticism, epistemic injustice, and the norms governing belief and inquiry.

Value Theory

Value theory is a broad category concerned with value, reasons, morality, and what people ought to do. It includes ethics, political philosophy, philosophy of law, aesthetics, and theories of well-being.

Ethics asks what actions are right or wrong and what obligations people have toward one another. Political philosophy studies justice, liberty, equality, authority, and political legitimacy. Aesthetics examines art, beauty, taste, and aesthetic value. More generally, value theorists investigate what makes something good, bad, valuable, or worth pursuing.

Philosophy of Language and Logic

Philosophy of language studies meaning, reference, communication, and the relationship between language, thought, and the world. It asks questions such as: How do words refer to objects? What determines the meaning of a sentence? How does context affect what someone says? How do speakers communicate things that go beyond the literal meanings of their words?

Philosophy of logic examines the nature and foundations of logical reasoning. It asks what makes an argument valid, what logical consequence is, whether there is a single correct system of logic, and how logic relates to truth, language, mathematics, and reasoning.

Philosophy of Science

Philosophy of science studies the methods, concepts, and assumptions of the sciences. It asks questions such as: What makes an explanation scientific? What is a scientific law? What is causation? Do scientific theories describe the world as it really is, or are they mainly useful tools for prediction?

The field also includes more specialized areas such as philosophy of physics, philosophy of biology, philosophy of psychology, philosophy of cognitive science, and philosophy of the social sciences.

History of Philosophy

History of philosophy studies the ideas, arguments, and intellectual contexts of philosophers from earlier periods. It includes ancient philosophy, medieval philosophy, early modern philosophy, nineteenth-century philosophy, and the history of more recent philosophical traditions.

Work in the history of philosophy often involves interpreting philosophical texts, reconstructing arguments, understanding historical concepts, and determining how a philosopher's views developed over time. Historians of philosophy may also use historical ideas to contribute to contemporary philosophical debates.

Feminist Philosophy, Philosophy of Race, and Social Philosophy

These fields examine the philosophical significance of gender, race, social identity, power, oppression, social institutions, and social relations.

Feminist philosophy investigates questions about gender, inequality, embodiment, knowledge, agency, and political structures. Philosophy of race examines the nature of race, racial identity, racism, and racial classification. Social philosophy more broadly studies phenomena such as social norms, recognition, domination, marginalization, collective responsibility, and structural injustice.

These areas are highly interdisciplinary and often overlap with ethics, political philosophy, epistemology, metaphysics, and philosophy of language.

A Note on Classification

This classification is intentionally very broad. Contemporary philosophy contains many additional subfields, including philosophy of action, philosophy of religion, philosophy of mathematics, philosophy of technology, philosophy of education, environmental philosophy, and philosophy of artificial intelligence. Many philosophical questions also belong to more than one area, so the boundaries between these categories are not strict.

Writing a Philosophy Paper

Writing a philosophy paper can be relatively straightforward for some people but challenging for others. I don't have much advice to offer on philosophical writing, as I'm still a student myself and have a lot of room for improvement in this area.

Resources on writing a philosophy paper:

A Gentle Reminder

Learning to read and write philosophy can be challenging, and progress does not always come as quickly as we hope. Please remember not to put too much pressure on yourself. If your results do not meet your expectations, that does not necessarily mean you have not worked hard enough. Please take a moment to recognize and celebrate the effort you have put in. Your hard work still matters, regardless of the outcome :).
